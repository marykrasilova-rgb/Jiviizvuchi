Improvisation, voice and expressive arts: five studies

Plain-language notes on what was studied, what the evidence cannot yet prove, and how each finding can inform embodied sound and improvisation practice.

Curated and interpreted by Maria Krasilova · composer · Playback Theatre musician with 10 years of experience · Miaphonia Voice Festival workshop leader in Georgia · Russia–Israel · 15 September 2026

Listening & co-learning

1. Another learner’s presence may speed up early sound learning

Finnish · 15 September 2026 · University of Jyväskylä

After four hours of training, 42 adults improved their perception of Mandarin tone contours. Participants learning beside another person progressed faster on day one, even without direct collaboration; this is an early effect, not proof that every group setting improves learning.

Practice connection. Two people can listen to the same contour side by side, then reflect it through voice and movement without “right or wrong” correction.

Intermodal expressive arts

2. Expressive arts therapy, psychological flexibility and self-acceptance

English · June 2026 · University of Hong Kong / JoCAT

Eleven women caring for frail older relatives moved between felt sense, movement, music and visual art. Qualitative observations suggest new ways of relating to difficult experience, but the small sample and limited quantitative differences do not support strong effectiveness claims.

Practice connection. When voice feels blocked, experience can move into gesture, line, colour or an object, then return to sound in a new form.

Improvisational music therapy

3. Improvisational music therapy in an immersive virtual space

English · 11 March 2026 · Human Behavior and Emerging Technologies

A multidisciplinary team tested whether VR can support remote non-verbal musical interplay. This was a feasibility study, not clinical proof; gaze, gesture, interpersonal distance and human professional interpretation remained central.

Practice connection. A voice journal or online session should preserve more than audio: pauses, movement, distance and a participant’s choice to approach, withdraw or remain in their own space matter.

Online singing & connection

4. What changes when an online singing group can truly hear one another

English · 27 January 2026 · Music & Science

A pilot mixed-methods study compared standard muted online singing with low-latency synchronous audio. Both formats were followed by better mood and connection and lower perceived pain and cortisol; synchronous sound produced a significantly greater gain in social connection, but the sample was small and the condition order was not randomised.

Practice connection. Shared audible sound is part of artistic contact. A muted mode can still offer a useful entry point for someone who is not yet ready to be heard.

Creativity, play & ageing

5. IMPROAGING: improvisational theatre as a multicomponent practice

English / Spanish · July–August 2026 · Gaceta Sanitaria

The programme involved 56 people with a mean age of 76.6. Some measures of movement, verbal and semantic flexibility, quality of life, loneliness and depressive symptoms changed after the programme; because the design was quasi-experimental, the findings are promising but do not establish causation.

Practice connection. My 10 years as a Playback Theatre musician have shown me this same layered structure: body, story-listening, partner response, play and creating a new answer in the moment.
